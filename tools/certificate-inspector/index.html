Certificate Inspector

Paste an X.509 certificate or a PKCS#10 certificate signing request to read it in plain language: subject and issuer, validity window, public key, signature algorithm, every extension, and the fingerprints. Review what the certificate is actually claiming, check a chain and its signatures, and export a JSON report. Parsing and cryptography happen in your tab — certificates never leave the page.

1 · INPUT

Paste one or more PEM blocks, a PEM chain in any order, raw DER as base64, or raw DER as hex. Private keys are recognised and refused on purpose. You can also drop a .pem, .crt, .cer, .der, .csr or .req file onto this pane.

2 · PARSED BLOCKS

Nothing parsed yet. Paste a certificate or load one of the samples to begin.

3 · OVERVIEW

Select a block to see its details.

4 · SECURITY REVIEW
  • Decode a certificate or request to see its review.

The review flags expired or not-yet-valid dates, broken or weak signature hashes, undersized keys, a CA that cannot sign certificates, long lifetimes and missing modern extensions. It is guidance about the certificate's own contents — not a statement that a chain is trusted.

6 · CHAIN & SIGNATURES

Every certificate in the input is ordered into a leaf-to-root chain using authority key identifiers, then each link's signature is checked against the issuer's public key with the Web Crypto API. A signature that verifies proves the issuer really signed that certificate — it does not prove the issuer is one you trust.

Load two or more certificates to build and verify a chain.

X.509 REFERENCE
What a certificate is
A DER-encoded ASN.1 structure — a SEQUENCE of three parts: the tbsCertificate (everything the issuer signed), the signature algorithmIdentifier, and the signature BIT STRING. PEM is the same bytes, Base64-encoded between -----BEGIN CERTIFICATE----- markers.
Fingerprints
The SHA-1 and SHA-256 digests of the whole DER certificate. A fingerprint identifies an exact certificate — change one byte and every fingerprint changes — but it says nothing about whether the certificate is trustworthy.
Subject and issuer
Distinguished names built from attribute type/value pairs. The common ones are C (country), ST (state), L (locality), O (organisation), OU (unit) and CN (common name). Modern TLS ignores CN and reads the hostnames from subjectAltName.
Validity
Two UTCTime or GeneralizedTime values, notBefore and notAfter. Certificates outside that window are rejected regardless of signature. Publicly-trusted TLS certificates are capped at 398 days.
Key usage
Which operations the key may perform — digitalSignature, keyEncipherment, keyCertSign and so on. extendedKeyUsage narrows the purpose further, for example serverAuth or clientAuth.
Basic constraints
Whether the certificate is a CA (CA: TRUE) and, optionally, how many intermediate CAs may follow it (pathLenConstraint). A leaf certificate should never be a CA.
Subject / authority key identifier
The subject key identifier is a hash of the public key; the authority key identifier names the issuer's key. Chain-building matches one to the other, which is why it works even when the same CA issues under several names.
Signature verification, and its limits
The tool checks that the signature over the tbsCertificate matches the issuer's public key. That is one link in a chain of trust. It does not check revocation, does not fetch anything over the network, and cannot tell you whether the root is one your system trusts.
